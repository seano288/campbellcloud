# p6: leave three.js, test a different engine (Babylon.js 9.33)

**Verdict up front.** The pictures clearly beat the repo's existing three.js stages, but most of that gain came from content I wrote (bevelled geometry, a wear shader, a lamp-matched reflection map, floor contact/reflection), not from the engine. Babylon saved real time on post-processing, but no image here shows something three.js could not do. I would not take on a second engine for this alone. The honest test is p6 next to the three.js prototypes (p2, p5): if those match it, drop Babylon. I did not build a like-for-like three.js scene, so this verdict is a judgement, not a measurement.

Own realism score: 6/10 on wide and impact shots, 5/10 on the close-up.

## Technique in plain language
Everything on screen is generated by code plus a few CC0 photo textures; there are no modelled assets.
- **Geometry** (`src/geo.ts`, `src/bots.ts`): a small hard-surface kit makes chamfered laser-cut plates, lathed parts, bolts, weld beads with a stack-of-dimes ripple, hoses and chain. Each vertex stores how close it is to an edge.
- **"Smart material"** (`src/wear.ts`): a Babylon MaterialPlugin injected into its stock PBR shader. It layers paint, primer and bare steel, and breaks through at edges and chips, then adds dust and grime. It also projects battle scars in the bot's own coordinates so a gouge runs across several parts, and shows live damage spots at each hit.
- **Floor**: one unique 4K steel map generated in Node with plate seams, bolts, tyre arcs, gouges and scorches. A second plugin adds soft contact shadow under each bot (a steel floor shows reflections, not diffuse shadow, so shadow maps alone did not ground them) plus a blurred planar reflection.
- **Light**: CC0 foundry HDRI dimmed to a dark hall, with the arena's real lamp grid painted in and baked to a prefiltered `.env`, used with box projection. Four overhead spots (two with shadows), a floor-bounce hemisphere light, and an impact flash.
- **Camera and finish**: TV-style cuts, long lenses, handheld drift, shake on hits, one angle through the wall. Babylon DefaultRenderingPipeline: 4x MSAA, ACES tone map, colour curves, bloom, grain, chromatic aberration, vignette, sharpen, DoF, plus half-res SSAO2 and object motion blur.
- **Sparks and smoke** (`src/fx.ts`): pure functions of fight time, so `?shot=` is exact. Sparks are drawn head-to-tail over a 1/48 s shutter. This means I did **not** use Babylon's GPU or node particles: they are not deterministic, which the brief's frozen shots need.
- **Fight** (`src/fight.ts`): keyframed and baked at 120 Hz, not physics. ~30 s loop, TORQUE always wins. Above about 70 rad/s the drum teeth swap to a translucent swept band.

Controls: `?shot=<s>`, `?q=ipad|high|low`, `?scale=`, `?nodof`, `?nomb`, `?ssr`, `?noadapt`, `?cam=x,y,z,lx,ly,lz,fov`. Three-step adaptive quality: DoF off, then motion blur off, then lower resolution, after 3 s below 28 fps. **Adaptive step-down is lightly tested:** it fired in bench runs under GPU contention and dropped resolution to 907x630. It only steps down and never recovers. The FIRE button (second priority) is not built.

## Engine choice
Compared: Babylon.js 9.33.2, PlayCanvas 2.23.3. Picked **Babylon**.

| | Babylon 9.33 | PlayCanvas 2.23 |
|---|---|---|
| Screen-space reflections | Yes (SSR pipeline) | Not in the engine's render passes (checked the npm source) |
| Object motion blur | Yes | Not in the engine source |
| Material shader injection | Material plugins, documented | Chunk overrides |
| Fit for this job | No lightmapper | Lightmapper and mobile focus, but much of that targets static scenes |

PlayCanvas's main strengths (lightmaps, many clustered lights) suit a static scene with lots of lights. This one has a few big lights and moving subjects, so the post-processing features mattered more. Not evaluated in depth: Filament, Godot web export, Unity web. All three felt like poor fits for a repo of small TS games, but that is an opinion, not research. I did not build a PlayCanvas scene, so its visual ceiling is untested.

**Babylon features that earned their place:** material plugins, prefiltered `.env` with box projection, MirrorTexture blur, SSAO2, DoF, object motion blur, MSAA on post, instances, anisotropy on the drum.
**Tried, no clear gain:** SSR. In `?q=high` it adds a slightly truer ANVIL reflection in the floor, barely visible next to the blurred mirror. That tier runs at about half the default's fps, though it also raises SSAO, shadow and mirror quality, so SSR's own share of that cost was not isolated. It is off by default.
**Not used:** IBL shadows, clustered lights, volumetrics, GPU/node particles, area lights. The brief's claim about these features was not tested here. Only WebGL2 is built: the plugins are GLSL only, so WebGPU would need WGSL ports.

## Cost of adopting a second engine
- **Bundle** (gzip, minimal esbuild test scenes): three r186 with composer and bloom 138 KB (554 KB raw); PlayCanvas with CameraFrame 547 KB (2.15 MB raw). Babylon as used here: main chunk 300 KB; all 221 JS chunks 761 KB gz, 2.97 MB raw. About 56 chunks are WGSL that WebGL2 never loads, and many are lazily loaded shaders. A PWA must precache all of them to work offline, so that means a precache entry per chunk, which is awkward.
- **Shared code**: nothing carries over from the existing three.js stages. Both engines have their own materials, loaders, math and cameras, and Babylon defaults to a left-handed Y-up system. The plain-JS parts (geometry data, fight timeline, texture tools) could be ported, but `src/garage`-style stage helpers could not. Two engines mean two sets of habits and two upgrade cycles for one family site. Babylon also ships frequent releases.
- **Types**: Babylon ships its own `.d.ts`, so setup is easy. This prototype passes non-strict `tsc` and is not held to the repo's lint or strict settings. It leans on `as any`.
- **Install**: `@babylonjs/core` unpacks to 73 MB on disk (`npm info`), with deep per-feature imports needed for tree shaking; missing side-effect imports fail at runtime.

## Measured
Machine: Apple M3 MacBook Air. Final numbers were taken in one pass with no other Playwright browser running at the start of each run (load average about 3-5). Adaptive quality was off (`?noadapt`). Each run was one fresh browser at 1180x820 (~1 MP, one CSS pixel per render pixel), sampled for 26-32 s across a live fight. The machine stayed shared, so another job could have started mid-run; nothing in the numbers suggests one did. These are still a laptop, not an iPad.

| Mode | fps avg | min | p10 | Notes |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| Chromium Metal, `q=ipad` (default) | 65.6 | 13.3 | 60.7 | Full chain. The minimum looks like a one-off dip |
| Chromium Metal, `q=ipad&scale=1.5` (1770x1230) | 31.9 | 25.9 | 28.5 | ~2.2 MP |
| Chromium Metal, `q=high` | 34.8 | 28.2 | 32.5 | SSR on, plus full-res SSAO and bigger shadow maps and mirror |
| Chromium Metal, `q=ipad&nodof&nomb` | 63.3 | 47.8 | 53.1 | Not faster than full chain in this pass, so the cost of DoF and motion blur is not settled |
| **WebKit 26.6 (Playwright, Apple GPU), default** | 49 | 12.7 | - | 32 samples; capped at 60. One dip to 12.7, the rest 38-60 |

Numbers vary a lot between runs. The same default mode read 90 avg in one run at 13:50, and 36 and 66 in others, so treat these as rough. Runs with another Playwright job on the GPU were much lower (the 36 run may be one of these; I did not confirm), and that is why they are not used.

Draw calls 313-323 (all passes). Scene geometry 216,000 triangles in 83 meshes plus 46 instances; the engine's active-index count reads about 740,000-760,000 with DoF on and 536,000 without. WebKit load to ready: 9.2 s on localhost under load, not re-measured idle. `tsc` non-strict: 0 errors.

Dist was tested from a nested sub-path on a plain static server. One 404 per page load remains; it is most likely `/favicon.ico`, because no favicon is shipped.

## iPad estimate (not tested on an iPad)
Based on the WebKit run above (about 49 fps on an M3 at 1180x820 with 60 as the cap), an M-class iPad should hold 30 fps or better at that resolution. It would not hold that at full Retina: 2.2 MP dropped Chromium to about 32 fps on this Mac. iPads have less cooling and thermal headroom than this estimate assumes, so expect some throttling in long sessions. Older A-series iPads will probably need the step-down tier. A 30 s reward is short, which helps. Two risks outweigh fps:
- **GPU memory**: textures decode to about 380 MB uncompressed (three 4K maps at ~89 MB each, five 2K at ~22 MB), the likeliest cause of a Safari tab crash. Production needs KTX2/ASTC and a smaller floor.
- **Shader compile stall**: 9 s on a Mac, likely longer on iPad. Needs a loading screen, and shader warm-up before the reward.

## Honest weak points
- Close up it reads as a good game asset, not a photo: surfaces are soft, teeth look clay-like, fine detail runs out, and drum flutes do not show.
- Wide shots lose it in the shapes: both bots are still simple, and ANVIL's large flat panels look clean.
- Hub and wheel faces go nearly black in some angles; the blue deck looks slightly lavender under the top lights.
- Motion is keyframed and light on weight. ANVIL's KO flip and the panel flight are simple arcs. Spinning-drum blur is a plain translucent shell.
- Smoke has no depth softening, so it can cut hard against geometry.
- Scars repeat their pattern on both sides of a bot. Polycarbonate is close to black, so scratched-plastic realism is weak apart from the through-wall shot; it reads as dark glass.
- Rebaking the environment needs `tools/_/env.hdr` copied into `public/tex/` and the dev server running. `tools/make-env.mjs` also needs the deleted raw HDRI.
